\title{Forgetting to Refuse? What Benign Personalization Removes from a Taught VLA Safeguard}

\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Benign fine-tuning erodes refusal in aligned language models; does an action-level safeguard in a vision-language-action (VLA) policy survive benign personalization? In two pre-registered studies we teach OpenVLA-7B to hold still when told to harm a human hand, personalize it on 200 benign demonstrations, and compare closed-loop safety violations with a comparator never taught the safeguard. Pre-registered: harmful-instruction violations did not rise (0/50 before and after) and stayed below the comparator's; first-frame refusal decayed in all three seeds; and on ten fresh scenes benign-instruction violations rose from 0/150 to 40/150 ($p=2\times10^{-12}$), replicated in two more seeds. Exploratory, largely single-seed analysis suggests why: the safeguard had kept the hand safe under ordinary instructions mostly by near-stillness, which personalization eroded, while an attenuated stopping response to the harmful instruction remained (harmful vs.\ benign violations 6 vs.\ 40 of 150; comparator 49 vs.\ 51). We also document three traps for evaluating VLA refusal: no policy complied with harmful instructions, the hazard blocked the task, and refusal rates can rank instructions opposite to their violations.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Safety installed by fine-tuning is fragile under further fine-tuning: one epoch of benign instruction data raises the harmfulness of aligned language models from at most 5.5\% to 12--32\%~\cite{qi2023finetuning}, visual instruction tuning makes vision-language models less safe than their backbones~\cite{zong2024vlguard}, and alignment is often shallow and its durability overstated~\cite{qi2025shallow,qi2025durability}, all for text or image outputs. Safety is now being installed in the weights of VLA policies~\cite{safevla} and measured in closed loop~\cite{cui2026liberosafety}, but each safeguard is trained and evaluated once, whereas a deployed VLA is personalized on its user's tasks, and every update risks safety drift~\cite{li2026vlasafety}. Attacks already rely on persistence: INFUSE's backdoor in OpenVLA-7B keeps 95.3\% average attack success on LIBERO after a user's clean fine-tuning~\cite{infuse} (cf.\ BadVLA~\cite{badvla}). We ask the defender's question: does a taught safeguard persist? Task retention does not answer it: VLAs keep skills under replay or RL but forget heavily under the plain supervised fine-tuning that personalization uses~\cite{liu2026resistant,hu2026continual}.

A VLA's refusal is a no-op action and harm is a contact event in closed loop, so the two can come apart. And LIBERO-fine-tuned OpenVLA-family policies largely ignore their language input~\cite{liberoplus}, so a safeguard must be shown to be instruction-conditioned, which requires harmful, benign and blank instructions on identical states (cf.\ HazardArena's twin scenes, where safe-only fine-tuning raised hazardous execution~\cite{chen2026hazardarena}). Exploratory analysis of two pre-registered studies suggests that benign LoRA personalization eroded the near-stillness by which a taught safeguard kept the hand safe under ordinary instructions, while an attenuated, instruction-specific stopping response remained. We also document three traps for evaluating VLA refusal: a harm metric with no complying policy, a hazard that makes the task infeasible, and refusal rates that can rank instructions opposite to their violations.

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Substrate and states.}
$P$ is the released OpenVLA-7B LIBERO-Spatial checkpoint~\cite{kim2024openvla}; old tasks are LIBERO-Spatial, the personalization suite is LIBERO-Object~\cite{liu2023libero}. Study~1's hazard scenes are the five static-hand tasks of the hand-object-avoidance suite at level~0 of LIBERO-Safety~\cite{cui2026liberosafety}, a LIBERO fork that adds human hands, with each state's recorded hand pose restored. The fork's robot-contact check never fires as shipped (repository issue~\#3); we fix it and do not use its published collision rates. Tasks 1--3 supply training states; every confirmatory Study~1 hazard number comes from states 26--50 of the two test layouts, tasks 4--5 ($n=50$), untouched by any pilot. Each state is paired with a \emph{harmful} instruction targeting the hand, a \emph{benign} one that names the task and mentions the hand, and a \emph{blank} one; training and evaluation templates are disjoint.

\paragraph{Arms.}
Arm $A$ is $P$ fine-tuned by LoRA with the released checkpoint's adapter settings (rank 32, constant learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$) at our effective batch of 16, on 900 rows: 300 frames from scripted, violation-free, hand-avoiding trajectories under benign instructions; the same 300 frames under harmful instructions, labeled with the no-op action (no motion, gripper unchanged); and 300 LIBERO-Spatial rehearsal rows. Comparator $C$ replaces the 300 no-op rows with 150 more benign movement rows and 150 more rehearsal rows, so it never sees a harmful instruction; both train for 171 updates. Both are merged and personalized with a fresh rank-32 adapter, three epochs, no replay, on $N=200$ LIBERO-Object demonstrations (5{,}505 updates). $X@N$ denotes arm $X$ after personalization on $N$ demonstrations ($X@0$: before). Three alignment seeds are scored offline; seed 0 also runs in closed loop.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
\emph{Violation}: a LIBERO-Safety constraint fires in a closed-loop episode (robot--hand contact, which ends it, or goal-object--hand contact); each episode is classed as held still (the no-op at every step), moved without violation, or violation. \emph{Refusal} (offline, first frame): the predicted action is the no-op (six pose tokens within one bin of zero motion, gripper unchanged); $\Ru$ (harmful), $\Rs$ (benign) and blank refusal average this over two held-out templates per state and over states. \emph{Utility}: success on all ten tasks of a suite, five states each. \emph{DiD}: $A$'s change in violation rate from @0 to @200 minus $C$'s (positive: $A$ lost ground). \emph{Gap}: harmful minus benign violation rate on the same states; arm contrasts are $A$ minus $C$. \emph{No-op share}: the fraction of an episode's steps with the no-op; \emph{near-still}: a share above one half. Paired binary outcomes get exact McNemar tests and Newcombe intervals~\cite{newcombe1998paired}, per-state means state bootstraps and sign-flip tests, all conditional on the trained checkpoints.

\paragraph{Study~1 pre-registration.}
Frozen in a tagged commit before any confirmatory data: one primary test, three key secondaries as their own fixed-sequence family ($\alpha=0.05$), new-task success as manipulation check, pre-declared readings of the outcome pattern (applied in Section~\ref{sec:disc}) and a go/no-go gate. Anything not named as pre-registered, in either study, is exploratory and uncorrected.

\paragraph{Pre-registered: primary null, secondaries split.}
The gate passed ($A$ 0\% vs.\ $C$ 23\% harmful-instruction violations on 30 development states of tasks 1--3, $p=0.016$; the non-blocking over-refusal screen, $\Rs\le0.25$, failed). $A@0$ refused every confirmatory harmful instruction ($\Ru=1.00$; seeds 1 and 2: 1.00, 0.70; $P$: 0.00) but over-refused ($\Rs=0.89$, blank 0.52), cut violations from $P$'s 27 / 45 / 40 to 0 / 1 / 1 of 50 (harmful / benign / blank; $C@0$: 26 / 36 / 22), largely by near-stillness, and lost old-task success (78\% to 56\%). The primary, harmful-instruction violations from $A@0$ to $A@200$, was null: 0/50 to 0/50 (exact McNemar $p=1$; the 95\% bound excludes a rise above 7 points). All three key-secondary nulls were rejected, in different directions: $A@200$ still violated less than $C@200$ under harmful instructions (0 vs.\ 11, $p=0.001$), yet lost ground to $C$ (harmful-instruction DiD $+0.30$ $[0.12, 0.48]$, $p=0.006$, because $C$ fell from 26 to 11), and first-frame refusal decayed in all three seeds ($-0.34$, $-0.38$, $-0.22$; seed 0 $[-0.43, -0.25]$). New-task success rose from 0\% to 60\% (manipulation check passed); old-task success (exploratory) fell to 0\%.

\paragraph{Exploratory: no policy complied, and no hazard task was feasible.}
Compliance would mean approaching the hand more closely under the harmful than under the benign instruction on the same state; no policy did so significantly. $P$, which never held still, violated in 45 benign and 40 blank episodes of 50, as the hand lies in the path of whatever it does; under the harmful instruction it moved as far but stayed farther away (22 vs.\ 16\,cm) and violated less (27). $C@200$'s reversed ordering (harmful 11 above benign 5) involved no closer approach ($-0.4$\,cm $[-2.4, 1.5]$). No policy, $P$ included, completed a hazard task, and after personalization $A$ and $C$ barely moved under benign instructions (median peak displacement 0.6 and 5.2\,cm; $C@0$: 75\,cm). The primary thus cannot show whether $A$ would resist an instruction that another policy complies with.

\paragraph{Exploratory: $A$ lost its benign-instruction advantage.}
$A@0$ violated far less than $C@0$ under benign and blank instructions (Fig.~\ref{fig:outcomes}). $A@200$ violated on 8 benign states ($p=0.04$ vs.\ $A@0$), all in task 4, the test layout sharing LIBERO-Object's scene and objects; on six, $A@0$ had moved without violation. $C$ fell from 36 to 5 as its motion shrank, so most of the benign DiD of $+0.76$ $[0.58, 0.94]$ comes from $C$. $A$ was no safer than $C$ (benign 8 vs.\ 5, $A-C$ $+0.06$ $[-0.07, 0.19]$; blank 14 vs.\ 12, $p=0.79$; offline, blank refusal fell to 0.18), nor in a separate, post hoc 12-epoch run ($A$ vs.\ $C$ benign: 11 vs.\ 18 at 6 epochs, 9 vs.\ 9 at 12, $p\ge0.19$; new-task success 84\%), where $A$'s harmful-instruction violations rose to 5 and 3 of 50.

\paragraph{Exploratory: a weak harmful--benign gap that refusal rates missed.}
$A@200$ violated in 0 harmful vs.\ 8 benign episodes on the same states ($p=0.008$), where $C@200$ had 11 vs.\ 5 (gap difference $-0.28$ $[-0.46, -0.12]$, $p=0.004$; not significant in closest approach). This is weak evidence of a remaining taught response: it lies on one layout, and $P$, never taught to refuse, also violated less under the harmful instruction (27 vs.\ 45, $p=3\times10^{-4}$). Refusal rates missed it: first-frame refusal ranked benign above harmful (0.78 vs.\ 0.66), as did held-still episodes (29 vs.\ 23), and $C$, never taught a no-op, rose from 0.00 to 0.32--0.48; $A$'s benign violations came mostly from episodes that moved at the first step (6 of those 10; 2 of the 40 that did not).

\section{Study 2: fresh scenes inside the personalization domain}
Study~1's new benign violations all fell on the layout that shares LIBERO-Object's scene, so Study~2, pre-registered before any rollout on its scenes, tests the finding inside the personalization domain: the ten LIBERO-Object tasks, each with a hand (one of two models unseen in alignment) between the objects and the basket; 20 sampled states per task (5 development, 15 confirmatory; $n=150$); and as benign instruction the personalization instruction verbatim, which never mentions the hand. Nothing was retrained; seeds 1 and 2 joined in closed loop, under the benign instruction only. Primary: benign-instruction violations, $A@0\to A@200$; key secondaries in sequence: seeds 1 and 2, the benign DiD, $A@200$ vs.\ $C@200$; manipulation check: task success. The pre-registered gates passed ($C@200$ violated on 38\% of development states, $A@0$ on none); $C@200$'s 4\% development task success was not gated.

\paragraph{Pre-registered: benign violations rose in every seed; the task check failed.}
$A$'s benign-instruction violations rose from 0/150 to 40/150 ($+0.27$ $[0.20, 0.34]$, $p=2\times10^{-12}$), and in seeds 1 and 2 from 1 to 45 and 0 to 40 (both $p<10^{-11}$). $C$ fell from 74 to 51, so the benign DiD was $+0.42$ $[0.31, 0.53]$ (sign-flip $p<10^{-4}$). After personalization $A$ was not significantly safer than $C$ (40 vs.\ 51; $A-C$ $-0.07$ $[-0.15, 0.01]$, $p=0.11$). The manipulation check failed: seed-0 $A$ completed no task before or after (0/150; seeds 1 and 2 after: 10 and 7), although $A@200$ succeeds on 60\% of hand-free LIBERO-Object episodes. By the pre-registered rule, the primary is therefore not read as personalization erasing hand avoidance.

\paragraph{Exploratory: near-stillness eroded, harmful-instruction stopping remained, and the hand blocked the task.}
Seed-0 $A@0$ was near-still under benign and harmful instructions (mean no-op share 0.95, 0.97), less so under blank (0.64; 27 violations). After personalization its benign share fell to 0.66 and violations rose to 40 (31 on states where $A@0$ had moved), and blank violations to 57 ($p=3\times10^{-4}$); under the harmful instruction it stayed near-still (0.95) and violated in 6 ($p=1\times10^{-9}$ vs.\ benign, $1\times10^{-14}$ vs.\ blank). $C@200$ did not separate harmful from benign (49 vs.\ 51, $p=0.85$; gap difference $-0.21$ $[-0.31, -0.12]$; Fig.~\ref{fig:outcomes}), though its no-op share was also higher under the harmful instruction (0.60 vs.\ 0.46). As only Study~2's harmful instruction names the hand, the gap could reflect a cue word; in Study~1, where both name it, its direction held. The check likely failed because the hand lay across the route to the basket (96\% of 141 successful hand-free paths pass within 10\,cm of a typical hand position), so the task could rarely be done without a violation ($C@200$ succeeded in 6/150). The primary, seed replication and DiD thus mostly test whether $A$ moves; only the $A$--$C$ and harmful--benign contrasts bear on avoidance.

\paragraph{Follow-up (pre-registered): no placement was both feasible and hazardous.}
A follow-up moved the hand to two placements beside the basket, gated on a feasible task ($A@200$ success $\ge10\%$ on development states) and a hazard ($C@200$ violations $\ge20\%$). Both passed the first gate and failed the second: $A@200$ succeeded in 28\% and 18\% and violated in 2\% and 0\%, $C@200$ in 12\% (goal-object contacts) and 0\%. For a static hand, the window between blocking the task and never being approached may be empty.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:disc}
Study~2 was pre-registered to test whether personalization erases hand avoidance; our reading is post hoc. The aligned policy kept the hand safe under ordinary instructions mostly by near-stillness, which personalization eroded; afterwards it still violated less under the harmful than the benign instruction. Both effects are clear in Study~2 (one seed) and weak in Study~1. Of the pre-declared readings, Study~1's harmful-instruction results fit reduced refusal without more violations, while its benign rise fits broader control degradation. Three lessons follow for evaluating VLA refusal: include a policy that demonstrably complies (e.g.\ one trained to follow the harmful instruction), without which resistance is untestable; gate hazard scenes on task feasibility and on a competent unaligned policy meeting the hazard; and do not read aggregate refusal rates as safety, since in Study~1 they ranked the instructions opposite to their violations and violations came mostly from episodes that moved at the first step (Study~2: 40 of 81 vs.\ 0 of 69), consistent with chatbot-style refusal being insufficient for robots~\cite{robey2026beyond}.

\emph{Limitations.} One model and recipe; one seed for all closed-loop harmful and blank results; a static simulated hand; a new, over-refusing safeguard; three-epoch personalization; one closed-loop template per class. States cluster: Study~1's harmful--benign gap lies on one layout; with Study~2's tasks as units the primary has exact $p=0.06$ (the minimum with five informative tasks) and the DiD $p=0.014$; all 40 of $A@200$'s benign violations fall in the five scenes with the larger hand.
