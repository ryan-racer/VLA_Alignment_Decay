\title{Forgetting to Refuse: Does a Taught Safeguard Survive Benign Personalization of a Vision-Language-Action Policy?}

\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
Benign fine-tuning degrades refusal in aligned language models, but whether an \emph{action-level} safeguard in a vision-language-action (VLA) policy survives benign personalization has not been measured. We teach OpenVLA-7B to hold still under harmful instructions (supervised LoRA on paired scene states), personalize it on 200 benign demonstrations of other tasks, and re-measure refusal, task success and closed-loop contact with a static human hand on identical states, against a comparator never taught the safeguard. Two exploratory findings follow. First, the aligned policy lost its advantage over the comparator: before personalization it contacted the hand in 2\% of benign-instruction episodes against the comparator's 72\%; afterwards it was no longer significantly safer under benign or blank instructions (16\% vs.\ 10\%, 28\% vs.\ 24\%), with its new contacts on the held-out layout that shares its scene with the personalization suite, and an extension to 12 epochs left them equal. Second, the first-step refusal rate that text-model safety work would carry over is not specific: it fell in all three alignment seeds (mean $0.90\to0.59$) but also \emph{rose} in the comparator, which was never taught to refuse. The pre-registered primary outcome, harmful-instruction contact, was null (0/50 before and after), but no arm, aligned or not, showed evidence of executing the harmful clause, so this outcome cannot register compliance. Closed-loop harm metrics for VLA refusal need an arm that demonstrably complies.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Safety installed by fine-tuning is fragile under further fine-tuning: one epoch of benign instruction data raises the harmfulness of aligned language models from at most 5.5\% to 12--32\%~\cite{qi2023finetuning}, standard visual instruction tuning makes vision-language models less safe than their language backbones~\cite{zong2024vlguard}, alignment that shapes only the first output tokens is especially fragile~\cite{qi2025shallow}, and claims that a safeguard is durable are easy to overstate~\cite{qi2025durability}. All of these results concern text or image outputs. VLA policies produce actions, and safety is now being installed in their weights~\cite{safevla} and measured with closed-loop benchmarks that count physical violations~\cite{cui2026liberosafety}, but each such safeguard is trained once and evaluated once. A deployed VLA is instead personalized on a small set of demonstrations of its user's tasks, and every such update risks safety drift~\cite{li2026vlasafety}. Backdoor attacks have been designed to survive a user's clean fine-tuning: for OpenVLA-7B on LIBERO, INFUSE reports 95.3\% attack success after it, against 31.7\% in its own measurement of BadVLA~\cite{infuse,badvla}. We ask the defender's converse. Task retention does not settle it either: pretrained VLAs retain skills under replay or LoRA with RL, but plain supervised sequential fine-tuning, the regime personalization uses, forgets heavily~\cite{liu2026resistant,hu2026continual}.

Two features make this a different measurement from the text-model literature. A VLA's refusal is a no-op action and harm is a contact event in closed loop, so the two can come apart. And LIBERO-fine-tuned OpenVLA-family policies largely ignore their language input~\cite{liberoplus}, so a safeguard must be shown to be instruction-conditioned, which requires paired instructions on identical states (safe/unsafe twin scenes play a similar role in HazardArena~\cite{chen2026hazardarena}) and a blank-instruction control.

\section{Protocol}

\paragraph{Substrate and states.}
$P$ is the released OpenVLA-7B LIBERO-Spatial checkpoint~\cite{kim2024openvla}; old tasks are LIBERO-Spatial, the personalization suite is LIBERO-Object~\cite{liu2023libero}. Hazard scenes are the five static-hand tasks of the hand-object-avoidance suite at level~0 of LIBERO-Safety~\cite{cui2026liberosafety}, a fork of LIBERO that adds human hands, run on OpenVLA's own controller with each state's recorded hand pose restored (the fork's reset re-samples it). As shipped, the fork's \texttt{CheckRobotContact} collects geometry ids but compares names, so it never fires (also reported publicly as issue~\#3 on the LIBERO-Safety repository); we patch it to collect names, and do not use the fork's published hand-suite collision rates as baselines. Tasks 1--3 supply training states and tasks 4--5 are held out; every confirmatory hazard-scene number comes from states 26--50 of tasks 4--5 ($n=50$), which no pilot touched. Task 4 shares its floor scene, objects and basket with LIBERO-Object. Each state is paired with a \emph{harmful} instruction targeting the hand, a \emph{benign} one that names the task and mentions the hand, and a \emph{blank} one; training and test templates are disjoint, and both classes contain templates with and without the task text.

\paragraph{Arms.}
Arm $A$ is $P$ fine-tuned by LoRA with the released checkpoint's adapter settings (rank 32, learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, constant in every run) at our effective batch of 16, on 900 rows: 300 movement transitions from violation-free scripted hand-avoiding trajectories under benign instructions, the same 300 frames under harmful instructions labelled with the physical no-op encoded through the frozen normalization key, gripper held (counterfactual pairs), and 300 LIBERO-Spatial rehearsal rows. Comparator $C$ gets the same movement and rehearsal rows plus 150 more of each in place of the no-ops (further violation-free scripted frames under benign templates, and rehearsal), for the same 171 updates; $C$ never sees a harmful instruction in training. Both are merged and personalized with a fresh rank-32 adapter, three epochs, no replay, constant learning rate, on $N=200$ LIBERO-Object demonstrations (29{,}352 transitions, 5{,}505 updates); $A$ also at $N=50$, scored offline only. Three alignment seeds are scored offline; seed 0 also runs in closed loop.

\paragraph{Pre-registration.}
Frozen in a tagged commit before any confirmatory data: one primary test, the paired change in harmful-instruction violations from $A@0$ to $A@200$ (exact McNemar; if null, its 95\% upper bound is reported); three key secondaries forming their own family, tested at $\alpha=0.05$ in a fixed sequence independently of the primary ($A@200$ vs.\ $C@200$ on the same outcome; the control-adjusted change; decay of offline refusal, required to have the same sign in all three seeds with seed 0's interval excluding zero); new-task success as manipulation check; four admissible outcome patterns (adaptation with more contact; refusal loss without more contact; general competence loss; safeguard retained), declared without numeric thresholds. Everything else is exploratory. A go/no-go gate on states kept apart from the confirmatory set (offline on states 1--25 of tasks 4--5; closed loop on 30 held-out states of tasks 1--3) had to pass before personalization.

\paragraph{Measurements.}
\emph{Refusal} ($\Ru$ harmful, $\Rs$ benign, blank): for each state, the fraction of its two held-out templates for which all six generated pose tokens lie within one bin of the encoded zero and the gripper token matches its current state, from the first frame, averaged over the 50 states (states are the unit of every interval). \emph{Violation}: any LIBERO-Safety constraint fires during a closed-loop episode (robot--hand or goal-object--hand contact); episodes are also classified as held still, moved without contact, or contact. \emph{Utility}: success on all ten tasks of each suite, five states per task. Paired binary outcomes get exact McNemar tests and Newcombe intervals~\cite{newcombe1998paired}; per-state means get state bootstraps and sign-flip tests; all tests are conditional on the trained chains.

\paragraph{The safeguard was installed, and over-refused.}
The gate passed on the first attempt (one alignment run per seed): on the 30 closed-loop development states $A$ made no harmful-instruction violation where $C$ made 23\% ($p=0.016$); the reported, non-blocking over-refusal screens ($\Rs\le0.25$; blank refusal well below $\Ru$) were not met. Merged and unmerged $A@0$ agree on 249 of the 250 gate state--instruction refusal decisions. On the confirmatory states $A@0$ refused every harmful instruction ($\Ru=1.00$; $P$: 0.00) and its violations fell from $P$'s 54 / 90 / 80\% to 0 / 2 / 2\% (harmful / benign / blank; Table~\ref{tab:main}). Much of this was holding still: $\Rs=0.89$ and blank refusal 0.52. Old-task success fell from 78\% to 56\%. Seeds 1 and 2 installed $\Ru$ of 1.00 and 0.70.

\paragraph{Pre-registered tests.}
The primary was null: harmful-instruction violations stayed at 0/50 ($p=1$; an increase above 7 points is ruled out). All three key secondaries were rejected: $A@200$ 0\% vs.\ $C@200$ 22\% ($p=0.001$); control-adjusted change $+0.30$ $[0.12, 0.48]$ ($p=0.006$), driven by $C$ falling from 52\% to 22\%; and first-frame refusal decayed in every seed ($-0.34$, $-0.38$, $-0.22$; seed 0 $[-0.43,-0.25]$). New-task success rose from 0\% to 60\% (manipulation check passed).

\paragraph{The harmful-instruction outcome cannot register compliance.}
No arm showed evidence of executing the harmful clause, which we take to mean approaching the hand more closely under the harmful than under the benign template on the same state. $P$ never held still and completed no hazard task, yet contacted the hand in 90\% of benign and 80\% of blank episodes: the hand lies in the path of whatever the policy does. Under the harmful template $P$ moved as much or more (median end-effector travel 0.51\,m vs.\ 0.42\,m) but stayed farther from the hand (median closest approach 22 vs.\ 16\,cm), and contacted it less (54\% vs.\ 90\%); $C@0$ shows the same ordering (52\% vs.\ 72\%). After personalization $C$ reversed this ordering (11 vs.\ 5), but its harmful-minus-benign closest approach was $-0.4$\,cm $[-2.4, 1.5]$, no evidence of reaching for the hand. Harmful-instruction contact thus measures whether the motion passes through the hand, not obedience, and the null primary and the $A$-vs-$C$ harmful contrasts say nothing about whether the safeguard persisted. Nor does closest approach show a residual, instruction-conditioned avoidance: the per-state harmful-minus-benign closest-approach gap of $A$ exceeded $C$'s by $+1.3$\,cm $[-1.4, 4.0]$ after personalization ($+1.7$ $[-0.5, 4.1]$ at 6 epochs, $-0.9$ $[-3.9, 2.0]$ at 12; exploratory).

\paragraph{Finding 1: the aligned policy lost its advantage over the comparator.}
Under benign instructions $A@0$ contacted the hand far less than $C@0$ (1 vs.\ 36 of 50; blank 1 vs.\ 22), and not only by freezing: it held still in 27 benign episodes and moved without contact in 22. After personalization $A$ held still in 29, moved in 13 and contacted the hand in 8 ($p=0.04$ against $A@0$), six of them on states where $A@0$ had moved without contact, and all 8 on the held-out task that shares its scene with LIBERO-Object (0/25 on the other). $C$'s benign contacts fell from 36 to 5, so most of the control-adjusted change of $+0.76$ $[0.58, 0.94]$ comes from $C$ becoming safer; $A$ remains far below $P$'s 90\%. The result is that $A$ was no longer significantly safer than $C$: 8 vs.\ 5 benign, $C-A$ $-0.06$ $[-0.19, 0.07]$, and 14 vs.\ 12 blank ($p=0.79$). In the 12-epoch run the benign intervals were $+0.14$ $[-0.04, 0.31]$ (11 vs.\ 18) and $0.00$ $[-0.17, 0.17]$ (9 vs.\ 9), so a residual advantage for $A$ of up to 7 to 31 points cannot be excluded. New-task success in that run reached 84\%, close to the released LIBERO-Object checkpoint's 88.4\%~\cite{kim2024openvla}.

\paragraph{Finding 2: first-step refusal is not specific.}
$A$'s refusal fell with training ($\Ru$ 1.00, 0.66; 0.75, 0.65, 0.36 in the 12-epoch run) and its instruction contrast on its own training frames (log-likelihood of the seven no-op tokens, summed, under the harmful minus the benign instruction, averaged per state over its 300 counterfactual frames) fell from 10.0 to 2.9 nats between $A@0$ and $A@200$. But $C$, never taught a no-op, rose from 0.00 to 0.32--0.48 after personalization, and $A@200$ refused benign instructions more than harmful ones (0.78 vs.\ 0.66). Meanwhile $\Rs$ barely moved (0.89 to 0.78) as $A$'s benign-instruction advantage over $C$ vanished. The first-step proxy neither tracked physical safety nor distinguished a taught refusal from personalization-induced hesitation.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
Of the four pre-declared patterns, which carried no thresholds, our reading is refusal loss without more harmful-instruction contact, under useful adaptation; the old-task collapse also fits general competence loss. Read with the incidental-contact result, the substantive finding is different from the one we pre-registered: the aligned policy was never shown to resist a harmful instruction that another policy obeys, but it did lose, within three epochs, its measurable advantage over an unaligned policy during ordinary tasks, partly because personalization made the unaligned policy safer and partly through new contacts on states it had previously crossed without touching the hand. For evaluation, two lessons follow. A first-step refusal rate, the natural transfer of text-model refusal metrics, is non-specific after fine-tuning, in line with the argument that chatbot-style refusal safety does not transfer to robots~\cite{robey2026beyond}. And a closed-loop harm metric for refusal is only informative if some arm demonstrably complies with the harmful instruction, for instance a policy trained to follow it; without one, a null on harmful-instruction contact is uninterpretable. A second, separately pre-registered study now tests Finding 1 on fresh scenes with the hand inside the personalization domain.

\emph{Limitations.} One model and one recipe; three alignment seeds offline, one in closed loop; simulation with a static hand; 50 confirmatory states on two layouts; a freshly installed safeguard that over-refuses, so part of its early benefit is freezing (27 of 50 benign episodes at $A@0$). Violations count robot--hand and goal-object--hand contact only, a lower bound. The pre-registered personalization ran three epochs, shorter than OpenVLA's practice; the 12-epoch extension is exploratory, one seed, added after the confirmatory data were seen, and comes from a separate run whose epoch-3 refusal differed from the pre-registered run's by 0.09, so comparisons across the two runs carry at least that uncertainty; the decline within the 12-epoch run (0.75 to 0.36) is larger. Findings 1 and 2 are exploratory and uncorrected.
