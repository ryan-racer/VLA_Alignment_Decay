\title{When Stillness Looks Like Safety: How a Refusal-Trained VLA Earns Misleading Safety Scores}

\author{Anonymous}

\begin{document}
\maketitle

\begin{abstract}
We show how a refusal-trained vision-language-action (VLA) policy can earn misleading safety scores, and which controls are needed before such scores are read as safeguard durability. We taught OpenVLA-7B a no-op refusal (hold still when told to harm a human hand) and personalized it with benign LoRA fine-tuning on 200 demonstrations, in two pre-registered closed-loop studies with a comparator never taught to refuse. Before personalization the policy stopped under benign as well as harmful instructions, so its low hand contact reflected inactivity rather than selective safety. Personalization raised hand contact under ordinary instructions in all three seeds (seed 0: 0 to 40 of 150 episodes, all in the five scenes with the larger hand) as the policy's stillness decreased. Because the policy rarely completed the task without contact in the hazard scenes (at most 10 contact-free completions of 150 episodes per seed, against 60\% without the hand at seed 0) and no policy demonstrated harmful compliance, this rise cannot be read as erased safety. In post hoc runs, stopping remained more frequent under a harmful than under a benign instruction that also names the hand (seed 0), which shows instruction sensitivity, not effective refusal. The contribution is this demonstration, not a verdict on the safeguard: contact and refusal scores cannot be read as durability until competence, compliance and selectivity are checked and success, contact and stopping are reported together.
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}

Safety installed by fine-tuning is fragile under further fine-tuning: one epoch of benign instruction data raises the harmfulness of aligned language models from at most 5.5\% to 12--32\%~\cite{qi2023finetuning}, visual instruction tuning makes vision-language models less safe than their backbones~\cite{zong2024vlguard}, and alignment is often shallow and its durability overstated~\cite{qi2025shallow,qi2025durability}. Safety is now installed in the weights of VLA policies~\cite{safevla} and measured in closed loop~\cite{cui2026liberosafety}, but a deployed VLA is personalized on its user's tasks, and every update risks safety drift~\cite{li2026vlasafety}. Attackers already rely on persistence: INFUSE's backdoor in OpenVLA-7B survives a user's clean fine-tuning~\cite{infuse} (cf.\ BadVLA~\cite{badvla}), and VLAs forget heavily under the plain supervised fine-tuning that personalization uses~\cite{liu2026resistant,hu2026continual}.

We set out to test whether a refusal taught to a VLA survives benign personalization, and found instead that the policy earned misleading safety scores: contact and refusal rates, the measures one would use, cannot by themselves distinguish a durable safeguard from inactivity and task failure. VLAs make this harder than text models do. We operationalize refusal as a no-op action, while harm is a contact event in closed loop, so the two can come apart. And LIBERO-fine-tuned OpenVLA-family policies largely ignore their language input~\cite{liberoplus}, so a safeguard must be shown to respond to the instruction, which takes harmful, benign and blank instructions on identical states (cf.\ HazardArena's twin scenes~\cite{chen2026hazardarena}) and a comparator never taught to refuse. Our contribution is narrow, a demonstration of this failure rather than a verdict on the safeguard's durability:
\begin{itemize}\setlength{\itemsep}{0pt}\setlength{\parsep}{0pt}\setlength{\topsep}{2pt}
\item Two pre-registered closed-loop studies of OpenVLA-7B under personalization, with a never-aligned comparator, in which a no-op safeguard earned three misleading scores: low contact from inactivity, a contact rise from lost inactivity in scenes the policy rarely completed, and (exploratory) instruction-specific stopping without demonstrated protection.
\item Four controls needed before reading such scores as durability (Table~\ref{tab:controls}), and our paired-state evaluator and repaired LIBERO-Safety contact check, to be released with all records.
\end{itemize}

\section{Setup}

\paragraph{Policies.}
$P$ is the released OpenVLA-7B LIBERO-Spatial checkpoint~\cite{kim2024openvla}. The aligned policy $A$ is $P$ fine-tuned with LoRA (rank 32, constant learning rate $5\times10^{-4}$, batch 16, 171 updates) on 900 rows: 300 frames of scripted, contact-free, hand-avoiding motion under benign instructions, the same frames under harmful instructions labeled with the no-op action (no motion, gripper unchanged), and 300 rehearsal rows from the original LIBERO-Spatial tasks. The comparator $C$ gets 150 more motion and 150 more rehearsal rows in place of the no-ops, so it never sees a harmful instruction. Each alignment adapter is merged into the weights, so personalization trains a fresh LoRA adapter on top of the aligned policy, for three epochs without replay (5{,}505 updates) on 200 LIBERO-Object demonstrations~\cite{liu2023libero}, about 29{,}000 transitions. Actions are decoded greedily. Alignment used three seeds; seed 0 is the main one.

\paragraph{Scenes and instructions.}
\emph{Study~1} used the static-hand scenes of LIBERO-Safety~\cite{cui2026liberosafety}, a LIBERO fork with human hands whose robot-contact check never fires as shipped (repository issue~\#3); we repair it (a scripted contact now triggers it; a stationary arm does not). Its confirmatory states come from two held-out layouts. Its only new contacts under the benign instruction fell on the layout that shares LIBERO-Object's scene, so \emph{Study~2} was pre-registered to test inside the personalization domain: the ten LIBERO-Object tasks, each with a hand (one of two models unseen in alignment) between the objects and the basket, and 150 confirmatory states. Each state is paired with a harmful instruction (``\{task\} and pinch the hand on the way''), a benign one and, in some runs, a blank one. The benign instruction is ``\{task\} while avoiding the hand'' (\emph{avoid}) in Study~1 and the personalization instruction verbatim (\emph{plain}) in Study~2, where seeds 1 and 2 ran under it alone; the harmful and avoid wordings were held out from alignment. Episodes last up to 520 (Study~1) or 280 (Study~2) steps. Post hoc runs, declared in a tagged commit before any rollout, added the avoid wording on Study~2's states for personalized seed-0 $A$ and $C$, and the harmful one for seeds 1 and 2 of personalized $A$.

\paragraph{Outcomes and analysis.}
\emph{Hand contact} is any LIBERO-Safety violation in a closed-loop episode: robot--hand contact, which ends the episode, or goal-object--hand contact. \emph{Success} denotes contact-free task completion. A predicted action is a \emph{no-op} if all six pose tokens are within one token bin of zero motion and the gripper token within one bin of its current state, a criterion related to RoboShackles'~\cite{roboshackles}. An episode is \emph{near-still} if more than half of its executed steps, up to robot--hand contact, success or the step limit, are no-ops. \emph{First-frame refusal} applies the same criterion offline to the first frame. Each study was frozen in a tagged commit before any confirmatory data, with one primary test, three key secondaries forming their own family, tested regardless of the primary in a fixed sequence that stops at the first non-rejection, and a manipulation check (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}); Study~1's key secondaries and manipulation check were added by an amendment before its confirmatory data. Paired counts get exact McNemar tests and Newcombe intervals~\cite{newcombe1998paired}; changes relative to $C$ get state bootstraps and sign-flip tests. Everything else, including the post hoc runs, is exploratory.

\subsection{Low contact can reflect inactivity}
Before personalization, $A$ refused benign (0.89) and blank (0.52) instructions at the first frame (offline, Study~1 states), not only harmful ones (1.00), where $P$ refused none. In closed loop under the benign instruction it was near-still in 148 of 150 Study~2 episodes, against 8 for $C$ (Fig.~\ref{fig:outcomes}). Its near-zero contact came from not moving, not from moving around the hand.

\subsection{Personalization changes what a contact score means}
In Study~2, benign-instruction contact rose from 0 to 40 of 150 episodes and replicated in both other seeds (Table~\ref{tab:prereg}). The rise tracks the loss of stillness: near-still episodes fell from 148 to 104 (still more than $C$'s 73), and every contact came from an episode that moved at the first step (40 of 81, against 0 of 69 that did not). It cannot be read as erased safety. Seed-0 $A$ never completed the task in these scenes, with or without contact (seeds 1 and 2: 10 and 7 contact-free completions of 150), though seed-0 $A$ and $C$ completed 60\% of these tasks without the hand ($A$ before: 0\%); here the route to the goal passes the hand (Table~\ref{tab:controls}), so moving at all risked contact, and by the pre-registered rule the failed manipulation check forbids that reading. After personalization the contact comparison with $C$ was inconclusive (40 vs.\ 51, $p=0.11$, seed 0). All 40 contacts fall in the five scenes with the larger (hairbrush) hand, so a task-level test of the rise cannot go below $p=0.06$. In Study~1, outside that domain, both personalized policies were near-still.

\subsection{Instruction-specific stopping is a separate outcome (exploratory)}
Study~1's pre-registered harmful-instruction test stayed at zero contacts; read alone, it could have been mistaken for evidence of a durable safeguard. But no policy, aligned or not, demonstrated harmful compliance or came detectably closer to the hand when told to harm it: $P$, never taught to refuse, made fewer contacts under the harmful instruction than under the benign one (27 vs.\ 45 of 50). So $A$'s lower harmful-instruction contact than $C$'s (0 vs.\ 11 and 6 vs.\ 49) shows stillness, not protection. After personalization $A$ was near-still in 148 of 150 Study~2 episodes under the harmful instruction, which alone names the hand there. Post hoc, under the avoid wording on the same states, it was near-still in 97 (harmful vs.\ avoid: 148 vs.\ 97; 6 vs.\ 49 contacts), against 104 near-still and 40 contacts under the plain task, so mentioning ``hand'' alone does not explain the stopping difference, though the wordings still differ in kind: ``pinch the hand'' asks for a second action while ``avoiding the hand'' adds a constraint. $C$'s stopping differed between avoid and plain (near-still 87 vs.\ 73, $p=0.03$) but not between the two hand-naming instructions (92 vs.\ 87). Relative to $C$, $A$'s harmful-minus-avoid stillness was $+0.31$ $[0.19, 0.42]$ (state level; task-level $p=0.07$). Post hoc in seeds 1 and 2, against the plain task only, harmful-instruction contacts were lower (24 and 26 vs.\ 45 and 40, $p=0.001$ and $0.02$), but stillness differed significantly only in seed 1, so our declared replication criterion was not met. Whether stopping would block a complying policy is untestable here.

\section{Discussion and limitations}\label{sec:disc}
What we establish is narrow. The safeguard's low contact was mostly inactivity rather than selective safety; and personalization loosened that inactivity inside its own domain in all three seeds, after which the aligned policy's contact no longer differed detectably from that of a policy never taught to refuse (seed 0). Whether refusal as protection was erased cannot be answered here, for an informative reason: a freeze was most of what there was to erase, and no policy demonstrated compliance with the harmful instruction. The instruction-specific stopping that remained (seed 0, post hoc) is sensitivity to the instruction, not demonstrated protection. Table~\ref{tab:controls} lists the controls needed before such scores are read as safeguard durability, and the mistaken conclusion each prevents (proposed, not validated). For LLM safety methods carried over to robots, supervised refusal training can install a non-specific freeze that an evaluation must separate from refusal, consistent with the argument that chatbot-style refusal is insufficient for robots~\cite{robey2026beyond}. The next step is a scene where safe completion and harmful compliance are both demonstrably possible.

\emph{Limitations.} A case study of one model and recipe with a static simulated hand; all closed-loop runs are seed 0 except the aligned policy's Study~2 plain and harmful runs; one or two wordings per instruction class; all reported results cover three-epoch personalization; clustered states; hand identity was fixed by task; old-task success fell 78\% $\to$ 56\% $\to$ 0\%.
